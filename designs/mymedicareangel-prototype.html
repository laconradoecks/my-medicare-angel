Medicare Explained: Parts A, B, C and D in Plain English

What Medicare covers, who qualifies, when to enroll, and how Medicare Advantage, Supplements and Part D drug plans fit together. Everything starts here.

What is Medicare?

Medicare is the federal health insurance program for people aged 65 and over, and for some younger people with disabilities. It is not one single plan. It comes in parts, and understanding those parts is the key to choosing well.

Part A
Hospital insurance. Covers stays in hospital, care in a skilled nursing facility, and some home health care.
Part B
Medical insurance. Covers doctor visits, outpatient care, preventive services and medical equipment.
Part C
Medicare Advantage. Private all-in-one plans that bundle Parts A and B, and usually drug coverage too.
Part D
Prescription drug coverage, offered through private plans approved by Medicare.

Who qualifies, and when?

Most people qualify at 65. Your first chance to enroll starts three months before the month you turn 65 and runs for seven months in total. Missing that window can mean paying more for the rest of your life, which is why the timing matters as much as the choice itself.

Good to know: if you are still working at 65 and covered through your employer, different rules may apply. This is one of the most common situations we help people with.

The choice most people face

Once enrolled in Parts A and B, most people either add a Supplement and a drug plan, or switch to a Medicare Advantage plan. Neither path is better for everyone. The right answer depends on your health, your budget, your doctors and where you live, and that is exactly the conversation a licensed agent is for.

Original Medicare: What Parts A and B Cover

The foundation of all Medicare coverage: hospital insurance and medical insurance, what each pays for, and what they leave out.

Part A: hospital insurance

Part A covers inpatient hospital stays, care in a skilled nursing facility after a hospital stay, hospice care, and some home health care. Most people pay no monthly premium for Part A, because they or a spouse paid Medicare taxes while working.

Part B: medical insurance

Part B covers doctor visits, outpatient care, preventive services like screenings and annual wellness visits, lab tests, and durable medical equipment. Part B has a monthly premium of [CURRENT PART B PREMIUM] for most people, and you generally pay 20 percent of the cost of covered services after a small annual deductible.

The important gap: Original Medicare has no yearly limit on what you can pay out of pocket, and it does not cover most prescription drugs, dental, vision or hearing. That is why most people pair it with a Supplement and a Part D plan, or choose a Medicare Advantage plan instead.

What you pay

Part A
Usually no premium. A deductible applies per hospital benefit period.
Part B
A monthly premium plus about 20 percent of covered costs, with no annual cap.

Medicare Advantage (Part C): HMO, PPO and SNP Plans Explained

All-in-one plans from private insurers that replace how you receive your Medicare benefits, often with drug coverage and extras built in.

How Medicare Advantage works

With a Medicare Advantage plan, a private insurer approved by Medicare bundles your Part A and Part B benefits into one plan, usually adds drug coverage, and often includes extras like dental, vision and hearing. You still have Medicare; you simply receive it through the plan. Premiums are often low or even $0, and every plan has a yearly out-of-pocket maximum, which Original Medicare alone does not.

The main plan types

HMO
You use the plan’s network of doctors and usually need a referral for specialists. Typically the lowest premiums.
PPO
More freedom: you can see out-of-network doctors at a higher cost, and referrals are usually not needed.
SNP
Special Needs Plans, designed for people with certain chronic conditions or who qualify for both Medicare and Medicaid.

The trade-off to understand

In exchange for the low premium and the extras, you generally agree to use the plan’s network and follow its rules. Whether that trade is worth it depends on your doctors, your medications and how much you travel, which is exactly what we help you weigh.

Medicare Supplement (Medigap) Plans, Explained Simply

Add-on insurance that pays the share of medical bills Original Medicare leaves to you, in exchange for a predictable monthly premium.

What a Supplement does

Original Medicare pays roughly 80 percent of covered medical costs, with no cap on the rest. A Medicare Supplement, also called Medigap, picks up some or all of that remaining share: deductibles, the 20 percent coinsurance, and hospital costs beyond Medicare’s limits. You keep the freedom to see any doctor who accepts Medicare, anywhere in the country.

The good news: plans are standardized

Supplement plans are named by letters, and every plan with the same letter has identical benefits by law. A Plan G from one insurer covers exactly what a Plan G from another does. What differs is the price, which is why comparing insurers matters more than comparing brochures.

Timing matters here more than anywhere: in the six months after you enroll in Part B, insurers must accept you regardless of your health. After that window, they can ask health questions and may charge more or decline.

What Supplements do not cover

Prescription drugs (that is what Part D is for), and generally not dental, vision or hearing. Most Supplement holders pair their plan with a Part D drug plan.

Medicare Part D: Prescription Drug Coverage Explained

How drug plans work, why the same medicine can cost different amounts on different plans, and why enrolling on time matters.

How Part D works

Part D plans are sold by private insurers approved by Medicare. Each plan has a list of covered drugs, called a formulary, organized into tiers: generics on lower tiers cost little, brand-name and specialty drugs on higher tiers cost more. The same medication can sit on a different tier in different plans, which is why the right plan depends on the exact medicines you take.

The late enrollment penalty

If you go without drug coverage for more than 63 days after becoming eligible, Medicare adds a permanent penalty to your premium when you do enroll. Even if you take no medications today, a low-cost plan can protect you from that penalty.

Medicare vs. Medicaid: What Is the Difference?

Two similar names, two very different programs. Here is the difference in plain terms, and what happens if you qualify for both.
Medicare
Federal health insurance based mainly on age (65+) or disability. The same nationwide, regardless of income.
Medicaid
A joint federal and state program based on income and need. Rules and benefits vary by state.

Qualifying for both

Many people qualify for both programs at once. If that is you, special Medicare Advantage plans called Dual-Eligible Special Needs Plans (D-SNPs) are designed for your situation and often include significant extra help. This is one of the most valuable and least understood corners of Medicare, and well worth a conversation.

Medicare for Veterans: VA Benefits, TRICARE For Life and Medicare

If you served, your benefits work differently. VA care, TRICARE For Life and Medicare fit together in specific ways, and getting the order right can save you real money.

VA coverage and Medicare are separate systems

VA health care covers you at VA facilities. Medicare covers you everywhere else. Many veterans keep both, because having Medicare means you are covered at any hospital or doctor, not only VA locations, and it protects you if VA priority groups or funding ever change.

Retiring After 65: Moving From Employer Coverage to Medicare

If you worked past 65 with employer insurance, you have a special enrollment window when that coverage ends. Here is how to land smoothly.

Your special enrollment period

When you (or your spouse) stop working or lose employer coverage, you get an eight-month window to enroll in Part B without penalty. But drug coverage follows a stricter clock: you have about two months to get Part D coverage in place. Acting in the first month keeps everything seamless.

Watch out for COBRA

COBRA does not count as employer coverage for Medicare’s purposes. Staying on COBRA past 65 without enrolling in Part B can trigger lifelong penalties. This catches many people; do not let it catch you.

The order of operations

Confirm your last day of coverage, enroll in Parts A and B timed to that date, then choose your path (Supplement plus Part D, or Medicare Advantage) so everything starts the day your employer plan ends. We coordinate all three steps routinely.

Can I Delay Medicare Part B If I Am Still Working?

Published [DATE] · Reviewed by a licensed agent

The short answer: if you or your spouse are actively working and you are covered by that employer’s group health plan, you can usually delay Part B without penalty. When the work or the coverage ends, an eight-month special enrollment window opens.

When delaying is safe

The employer coverage must come from current, active employment. Size matters too: for employers with 20 or more employees, the group plan pays first and Medicare can wait. Under 20 employees, Medicare generally needs to be in place at 65, because it pays first.

When it is not

Retiree coverage and COBRA do not count as active employment coverage. Staying on either past 65 without Part B is the most common and most expensive mistake we see, because the penalty is permanent.
